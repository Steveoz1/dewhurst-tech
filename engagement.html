What the review often finds

Why good pilots stall at the first site

The answer usually turns on one or two of these. They are examples, not the review: which of them matter in your case - and what else does - is what the five days are for.

01

The middle isn't funded

The vendor's contract ends at the trial - and the site's change process was built for proven equipment. Someone carries the step between them, without the budget or the mandate.

02

The OT work hasn't been scoped

The robot works. Joining it to the site's control systems (OT) and cyber-security rules (IEC 62443) is a project in its own right, often the longest one.

03

The evidence is the wrong kind

A certificate shows it's fit for purpose. Only time on a live site shows it's fit for service - and that's what an operator signs against.

04

Competence is assumed

Who is trained to run it, check it and stop it, at the second site - not just the first?

What we look at

Three lenses, one test

The three elements, asked as plain questions. Underneath each sits a structured set of checks, built up deployment by deployment - which is what makes this five days rather than five weeks. The report shows what we found and why it matters; the checks stay with Dewhurst.tech.

Total Awareness

Have we seen everything it touches?

The plant, the people, the procedures, the history - the things a demonstration never meets.

Integrated Signal Intelligence

Does the right signal reach the person who decides?

From the sensor, through the OT and control systems, to the work order and the person who acts on it.

Evolutionary Transformation

Can it be brought in step by step?

So a failure along the way is survivable - and the gain outlasts the person who championed it.

One test across all three: when something goes wrong - and something always does - does the plan survive it? Engineers call it safe-life ("it won't fail") or fail-safe ("when it fails, we're still in the game").
